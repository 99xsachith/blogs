A discussion framework for product makers, IP owners & service providers

What's rising, what's falling — and which of it is good

Generative AI is moving a lot of numbers at once inside a software business: delivery speed, code quality, gross margin, valuation, hiring. Up isn't automatically good, and down isn't automatically bad — the two questions are independent, which is exactly what this compass is built to keep separate in a live conversation. Switch the tab below to read the same forces from the seat of the industry at large, the company that owns the IP, or the firm that delivers services to it — the same sixteen or so numbers turn into a different set of decisions depending on which chair you're in.

The compass

Direction × Verdict

Read it as two questions asked of any metric: is it moving up or down, and is that movement good or bad. Each item is tagged ST for a mostly short-horizon effect, LT for something structural. Three lenses below read the same underlying forces from three different seats at the table.

Business parameters · industry-wide

Budget justification

  • Fund AI narrowly and instrumented, not broadly and aspirational — 95% of enterprise pilots return no measured ROI; the 5% that work are narrow, well-integrated deployments
  • Separate AI cost of goods (inference/compute) from R&D headcount in the budget — they behave differently and get cut differently under pressure
  • Require a named before/after baseline before any pilot graduates past a quarter

Jobs & workforce

  • Track entry-level hiring as its own line, not folded into general engineering headcount — it's the number moving fastest and furthest
  • Validate any AI-driven headcount plan against measured productivity, not survey sentiment
  • Decide deliberately whether you're keeping a junior pipeline — don't let it shrink by default

Revenue & profitability

  • Watch blended gross margin as AI features enter the mix — 52% for AI-heavy work versus 80–90% legacy SaaS is a real gap, not a rounding error
  • Track valuation and comps quarterly, not annually — multiples have re-priced by billions within a single news cycle
  • Report durability (data, workflow depth, retention) alongside feature velocity, not instead of it

Business parameters · software maker

Budget justification

  • Size AI infra spend against a named, measured baseline — the 5% of pilots showing real ROI report $2M–$10M in annual savings from narrow, well-integrated deployments, not broad rollouts
  • Track AI cost of goods sold as its own line against a blended gross margin floor, not folded into general R&D
  • Default to vendor-built, workflow-integrated tools over internal builds where you can — they succeed roughly twice as often in the data

Jobs & workforce

  • Keep a deliberate junior-hiring floor even as senior output rises — this quarter's saving is next decade's senior-bench gap
  • Put code churn and refactor share on the same dashboard as velocity, so quality debt can't hide behind a good throughput number
  • Re-validate any headcount reallocation against measured task time, not “we feel faster”

Revenue & profitability

  • Set a blended gross margin floor that prices in AI COGS explicitly before packaging a new AI feature
  • Report a “moat score” next to revenue — proprietary data, workflow depth, retention lift — the factors that actually earn an AI premium in diligence
  • Treat your valuation multiple as a quarterly variable to revisit, not an annual-planning constant

Business parameters · service provider

Budget justification

  • Justify AI tooling spend per delivery pod against fixed-price margin lift — fixed-price work already runs about 8 points higher operating margin than time-and-materials

Jobs & workforce

  • Report revenue growth against headcount growth explicitly each quarter — genuine transformation shows revenue decoupling from headcount, not just both shrinking together
  • Protect a minimum entry cohort on purpose — the junior tier is the apprenticeship system that produces tomorrow's architects

Revenue & profitability

  • Set an explicit target mix of fixed-price/outcome-based versus time-and-materials revenue and move it every year
  • Price AI-specialist work at its real premium (roughly 30–50% over baseline) instead of folding it into standard rate cards
  • Give AI services (implementation, modernization, agentic operations) their own P&L line rather than treating them as a feature of existing delivery

Business parameters · CEO

Budget justification

  • Fund AI narrowly against a named baseline before it becomes a headline in an earnings call — optimism (80% of CEOs) is running ahead of proof (95% of pilots with no measured return)
  • Separate the AI compute line from the headcount budget explicitly — they behave differently under pressure and get scrutinized by different people on the board
  • Budget the redesign itself — the apprenticeship-pipeline replacement, the new AI-supervision role — as a real, funded line rather than assuming it happens on its own

Jobs & workforce

  • Separate a real automation case from a hiring correction before it goes in a public layoff announcement — the credibility cost of getting this wrong compounds with every headline
  • Redesign roles around comparative advantage deliberately, rather than leaving the mix of full-potential and AI co-pilot roles to be decided ad hoc, team by team
  • Fund a deliberate successor to the apprenticeship pipeline your own restructuring may be thinning — the bill for skipping this doesn't arrive on this year's watch, but it arrives

Revenue & profitability

  • Bring the board a dollar-denominated, before/after number, not a sentiment survey — half of CEOs already say their job depends on getting this call right
  • Report revenue per employee as a real strategic scorecard next to growth, the way AI-native comparables already do
  • Be the one voice in the room turning “investing regardless of returns” into a specific, falsifiable claim about what this company's AI spend is actually supposed to buy

Short horizon vs. long horizon

Same forces, read by time

The compass above sorts by direction and verdict, and reads differently depending on which lens you had open. This cut sorts the same underlying forces by how long they take to matter — useful for deciding what to fix this quarter versus what to plan the org around, whichever seat you're in.

Now through next few quarters

Short horizon
Benefits
  • Real throughput gains for teams with strong testing and version-control foundations already in place
  • Prototyping and experiment costs collapse toward zero
  • AI tool pricing is falling as the market segments and competes
Challenges
  • Felt productivity gains outrun measured ones — don't plan headcount off a survey
  • New AI features arrive with a real, ongoing inference bill that dents gross margin immediately
  • Personal AI tool use is running well ahead of any sanctioned policy or spend visibility
  • Funding rounds and comps can re-price a business by billions inside a single news cycle

Two to three-plus years out

Long horizon
Benefits
  • Structurally leaner, more capital-efficient companies become the norm, not the exception
  • The falling cost to test an idea keeps widening who is able to found a software company
  • Usage- and outcome-based pricing tie what customers pay to value delivered, not seats occupied
Challenges
  • Today's thin junior pipeline is tomorrow's senior-engineer shortage
  • Duplication and skipped refactoring compound quietly into debt that's expensive to unwind later
  • Durable advantage increasingly requires owned data and workflow depth — a plain feature set keeps getting commoditized
  • Value concentrates toward whoever owns the workflow and the data, not whoever ships the most features

The operating-model shift

Why, underneath everything above

Every quadrant on every tab is a symptom of one underlying change: cost now has a second variable input alongside people, and it forces a company to rethink capacity, roles, and budgeting — not just buy new tools.

A third input in the production function

The old operating model

Two inputs
  • Cost = labor + fixed capital — once built, serving one more customer cost almost nothing
  • Capacity = headcount, planned annually and slow to move in either direction
  • Judgment was trained as a free byproduct of junior staff doing volume execution work

The operating model now

Three inputs
  • Cost = labor + fixed capital + AI compute — a real, usage-linked bill that behaves like a utility, not a headcount line
  • Capacity = a substitution curve between a slow-but-deep input and a fast-but-shallow one, managed deliberately
  • Judgment now has to be trained on purpose — the free apprenticeship pipeline shrinks along with the junior tier
Redesigning roles around comparative advantage

Not “what can AI do” — where is the gap between an AI-adequate outcome and a human-excellent one largest, and whose time should protect that gap?

Full-potential roles

Where the human/AI gap is largest
  • Leadership, strategic judgment, and accountability under ambiguity
  • Taste, and framing a problem nobody has specified yet
  • The trust a client or team places in a specific person

AI co-pilot roles

Where AI already closes most of the gap
  • Directing, verifying, and correcting AI output at volume
  • Elevated throughput on bounded, well-specified work
  • A genuinely new discipline — not just a faster version of the old junior job
Four things this changes about how a company looks at itself

Budgeting

  • Moves from an annual headcount exercise to a continuously monitored blend of one sticky input and one liquid one
  • Closer to how a manufacturer plans labor plus a commodity input cost than how software used to plan a fixed R&D budget

Capacity planning

  • Becomes an explicit substitution decision instead of an implicit one hidden inside tool procurement
  • Needs a named owner for the mix — right now most companies decide it ad hoc, tool by tool

Talent strategy

  • Has to fund a deliberate replacement for the old apprenticeship pipeline, not assume it still happens for free
  • The bill for skipping this doesn't arrive for years — which is exactly why it's easy to defer

Strategic self-assessment

  • Shifts from “how much can we scale headcount” to “how much leverage per person who holds real judgment”
  • Makes revenue per employee a real strategic scorecard, not just an efficiency stat

Because the AI half of this equation is available to every competitor on roughly the same terms, it can't be the source of durable advantage by itself. Two companies with identical AI spend end up with different economics depending on whether they bolted AI onto the old org chart, or actually redesigned capacity, roles, and budgeting around it.

What users now expect

The ground is shifting under the product, too

Every quadrant so far is about the builder's side of the ledger. But the baseline a product is judged against isn't fixed either — it's set by how AI-literate the person on the other end of the product has become, and that's moving faster than most roadmaps assume.

Fluency is compounding faster than trust

What's rising fast

Capability expectations
  • Weekly AI use among U.S. workers roughly doubled in under two years — 27% in Q2 2024 to 50% by Q1 2026, with daily use up from 4% to 13%
  • Adoption is no longer generational: Gen Z, Millennial, and Boomer usage are all climbing quarter over quarter, just from different starting points
  • The default posture shifts from “does this have an AI feature” to “why isn't this already agentic, personalized, and proactive”

What isn't rising as fast

Trust & enthusiasm
  • 79% of consumers still say humans understand them better than AI does, and 91% prefer a human over a chatbot for support
  • Skepticism and concern lead consumer sentiment toward AI (28% each) over being impressed (26%) or excited (18%)
  • Only 27% of consumers say AI features make a product more appealing — familiarity is outrunning approval
Expected as a baseline, not paid for as a premium

The same survey population that's rapidly getting AI-fluent is, in the same period, getting less willing to pay extra for it — which turns rising usage into a rising cost with no matching revenue line.

Willingness to pay is falling, not rising

  • 50% of U.S. smartphone owners now say they won't pay extra for AI features, up from 45% a year earlier
  • Fewer than 12% of iPhone owners and 4% of Samsung owners say they'd pay for an AI subscription specifically

Baseline vs. differentiator

  • AI moves from a marketed feature to assumed infrastructure — like search or spellcheck before it
  • Differentiation has to come from what the AI does with your workflow and data, not from the fact that it exists

Trust has to be designed, not assumed

  • Because skepticism lags fluency, interfaces need to show their work — sourcing, confidence, an easy path to a human
  • A feature that quietly works can still lose users if it never earns visible trust

One UX, uneven starting fluency

  • 31% of Gen Z and 19% of Boomers use AI at work — the same product serves both ends of that gap simultaneously
  • Onboarding and defaults can no longer assume a single starting level of comfort with the AI in the product

The uncomfortable combination is that users expect more from AI and expect to pay nothing extra for it, while trusting it less than the feature list implies they should. That's not a marketing problem to spend past — it's a product-economics problem: the inference bill for meeting a rising expectation has to be absorbed inside the existing price, earned back in retention, or it doesn't get built at all.
